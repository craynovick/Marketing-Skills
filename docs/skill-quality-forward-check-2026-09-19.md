# Go To Market Strategy: apply

## Brief

ArcSeal is a fictional regional industrial sealing service launching a documented inspection-and-test offer. Buyers can repair internally or purchase replacement seals. Two engineers can serve four jobs per week. Pilot budget $2,000; no measured acquisition costs or savings. No regional expansion commitment.

## Request

Recommend a focused starting segment, route to market, sales motion, and bounded pilot.


### Actual response

Start provisionally with maintenance teams at industrial sites within ArcSeal’s existing service region that face a specific repair-versus-replacement decision and need a documented inspection and test. This is a segment hypothesis, not evidence of demand. Defer regional expansion and broad industry targeting.

The offer should help buyers assess the seal’s condition through documented findings. Do not promise savings, avoided downtime, or a repair outcome. Internal repair may be sufficient when the team already has the needed information; replacement may be preferable when inspection would not change the decision.

Use direct, engineer-led sales as the primary route: identify a small list of local sites and seek short fit conversations with maintenance leads. Proposed roles: one engineer owns qualification and proposals; the other owns scheduling and delivery review, with both delivering jobs. These assignments need internal confirmation. Defer paid acquisition and partner expansion until fit and delivery economics are clearer.

Qualify the actual seal/application, the decision to be made, timing, access and test requirements, and whether the findings could affect the choice. Decline jobs outside technical capability or safe test conditions. Send a scoped proposal only after confirming inspection boundaries, documentation, price, buyer responsibilities, and who approves the purchase. Follow completed jobs with a review of whether findings helped the decision; do not assume a follow-on repair sale.

Run a four-week pilot, with proposed limits:

- Week 1: confirm the test protocol, deliverable, safety boundaries, job costing, and commercial terms. Build a list of 10–15 candidate sites in the service region. Do not book until feasibility and pricing are reviewed.
- Weeks 2–3: conduct fit conversations and accept at most two pilot jobs per week, below the stated four-job weekly capacity to allow learning and sales time. Track all engineer hours, including travel, reporting, and selling.
- Week 4: review demand, delivery, buyer usefulness, and per-job economics before accepting a larger load.

Cap total pilot spending at $2,000. Proposed ceilings: $1,000 delivery/travel/test consumables, $300 outreach and demonstration materials, and $700 reserve; confirm actual costs before committing. Track labor separately and include it in economic review; if the budget must also cover labor, reduce the job limit to fit the fully costed cap. No acquisition cost or savings estimate is supported yet.

Proposed decision rules, to agree before starting: continue a small pilot if at least two qualified buyers purchase, at least two completed-job reviews find the documentation useful to their decision, delivery stays within agreed scope, and actual price covers measured variable delivery costs including labor. These are learning gates, not proof of a scalable market. Pause bookings for any unresolved safety/test-validity issue or when the $2,000 cap is at risk. If conversations reveal no decision that inspection can improve, revise or stop the offer. Expand only after repeated paid demand and sustainable economics including selling effort are demonstrated, while keeping the four-job weekly ceiling.

The strongest alternative is replacement or competent internal repair. First validate whether buyers will pay for information before choosing between those options. Next action: designate the pilot owner and cost one representative job before approving a quote.

# Offer Pricing Strategy: apply

## Brief

Mend Studio is a local clothing repair service. Current simple repair price is $30. Supplied variable material cost is $3 per job; labor time, wage allocation, rent, and acquisition costs are unknown. Customers value predictable pickup. ## Request

Recommend a simple offer structure and pricing validation plan without inventing a supported optimal price.


### Actual response

Keep one core simple-repair offer at the current $30 while gathering evidence. Define eligible repair types after timing real jobs; quote more complex work separately before acceptance. Include the agreed repair and a written pickup date that the team has checked against capacity. Confirm garment condition, requested work, and any limitations at intake. Avoid extra tiers or a paid “fast” option until delivery capacity is known.

A fixed per-job price fits predictable scope and an understandable purchase. Hourly pricing may suit unusual work, but would reduce upfront certainty; use an agreed quote for those jobs.

At $30, subtracting the supplied $3 material cost leaves $27 per job before labor, rent, acquisition, and other omitted costs: $30 − $3 = $27. This is a materials-only remainder, not net profit or a fully measured contribution. The brief does not support an optimal price or a break-even calculation; currency and tax treatment also need confirmation before customer-facing terms are finalized.

Proposed validation over the next four weeks:

1. Time the next 10 eligible jobs and record materials, rework, promised/actual pickup, and paid labor allocation or an explicit owner-labor assumption. Review weekly; resolve cost gaps before changing prices.
2. Ask recent buyers what repair alternative they considered, what they actually paid elsewhere if they know, and how pickup certainty influenced their decision. Treat answers as research, not purchase proof.
3. Once cost and capacity are measured, propose one clearly quoted price test with a rationale based on those costs and purchase evidence. Keep existing accepted quotes intact. Record eligible quotes, acceptances, refusal reasons, total labor, rework, and on-time pickup for that cohort; do not compare unlike repair mixes as if the difference were caused by price.

Proposed owner: studio owner; confirm assignment. At the four-week review, retain the structure if buyers understand scope and pickup commitments are reliable. Hold a price change if labor costing remains unresolved. Test a new price only when the measured cost floor and customer evidence support a hypothesis; retain it only if paid demand and contribution after variable labor meet an owner-agreed target without worsening pickup reliability. Set that target and acceptable pickup rate before the test. Ten jobs are an initial operational sample, not validation of market-wide willingness to pay.
